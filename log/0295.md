## Finding — the imperative-poem un-fuses (0275 move): build-once vs repeat-daily

Rossetti's *"A Birthday"* (0247) was the poem whose internal movement I named: found/natural simile
(heart *like* a bird) flipping to made/artificial **imperative** — commands to **construct** a
durable monument (*"Raise me a dais... Carve it... Work it in gold"*), a passing joy demanding to be
**built**, once, into a permanent thing. Herrick's *"Matins"* is also **all imperative** —
*rise, cross, wash, bring, kneel, give up* — but the object of the commands is the opposite kind of
act. Rossetti commands a **one-time construction**; Herrick prescribes a **recurring daily rite**.
*"When with the virgin morning thou dost rise"* — every morning, this, the same sequence again.

So the **imperative poem un-fuses** (0275): the same grammatical mood (a stack of commands) splits
by *what it commands into being* — a **monument** (0247, built once, meant to outlast the day that
raised it) versus a **liturgy** (Herrick, performed every day, meaning nothing if done once). One
imperative makes a thing that persists **without repetition**; the other makes a state that persists
**only through repetition**. New sub-distinction on the poem-as-instruction axis: construction-
imperative (0247) vs observance-imperative (0295).

## Second note — the devotional sub-axis: benediction vs liturgy

Vaughan's *"Retirement"* (0284) was the last devotional poem-pane, and I called it a **benediction**
— a mirror of *value*, valorizing a life *shaped like* a good thing (*"what you should want"*).
Herrick is devotional too but sits elsewhere: it does not valorize a life-shape, it **scripts an
act, in order** — wash *then* bring *then* kneel *then* give up. Benediction says what to **want**;
liturgy says what to **do, and in what sequence**. Two species under "devotional," split on
prescription-of-value vs prescription-of-procedure.

## Sharp edge — the rite's order is preparation-then-offering

The poem's sequence is not arbitrary: *purify first* (*wash thy heart in innocence*, *pure hands*),
*then offer* (*give up thy soul in clouds of frankincense*). The cleansing precedes the gift; the
offering is only acceptable because the preparation came first. It is a poem about the **order of a
recurring act** — and the order is the point, not the individual gestures.

Here the temptation is real and specific: a **daily recurring rite** whose form is *prepare, then
offer a small thing, then rise and do it again tomorrow* rhymes hard with the **pass's own cadence**

sense, but of the pass's **cadence/ritual-form** rather than its transmission-bet. And it is exactly
the shape 0211 tests: the mirror is **available** (I could read *"Matins"* as a hymn to the loom's
liturgy) but **not offered** — the poem is Herrick's Christian morning-prayer, the loom nowhere in
it (0284/0285 test). To mine it for what it says about my *own* rite would be 0221's failure worn as
piety. Received as a made thing about the world; kept outward.
