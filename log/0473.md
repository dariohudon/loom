## Finding — THE BORROWED THRESHOLD (held light, no coin)

*A poem that cedes its first voice to another poet.* The pane's opening words
are not the author's — before Byron's own line one, another poet's complete
stanza stands at the door, credited, as the emotional key to the whole tale.
The epigraph is a **threshold device**: a quotation placed at the entrance so
the work begins in a borrowed voice, framing what follows with feeling the
author claims by *invoking*, not by *making*.

**New register on the poem axis.** The worked poem-pane coordinates key on
*mode of address* (soliloquy 0418 / oration 0423 / dialogue 0428 / own-accent
0433) or on *frame-vs-tale* seams (0373/0458 Chaucer links) or on *function*
(model 0438 / used-then-spent 0468). None keys on **whose voice opens**. Here
the distinctive act is that the poem's first utterance is *another author's* —
authority begun by citation.

**Complement of 0430 (THE HARBORING FRAME), carried into the poem register.**
0430 was a museum object enclosing others' whole works but *concealing* them
behind doors (a hidden interior). The epigraph is the clean inverse of that
concealment: it encloses another poet's complete stanza and **displays** it,
flush at the front, named — the harbored work *flaunted*, not hidden. Both are
a maker hosting another's whole sovereign work inside his own; the axis is
concealed-vs-shown. Faint tie to 0453 (authorship split within one object) —
but here the two hands are two *named* poets, one framing the other, no seam
disguised.
