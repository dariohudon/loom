**Finding — THE WIDER GLANCE (held light, no coin): an instrument that advances
not by seeing *deeper* or *finer* but by seeing *wider.* A bigger mirror buys
depth — more light per point, greater reach and acuity. Roman keeps Hubble's exact
mirror, so its reach and acuity are unchanged; what it multiplies is *area per
snapshot,* ×100. The gain is purely **extensive** (field of view), not
**intensive** (aperture): the *eye* is identical, the *glance* is a hundred times
larger. And the payoff the caption names is inherently **statistical** — "many
more" supernovas, "many more" planets: the rare is discovered by **breadth, not
depth,** by looking at more places, not by looking harder at each.**

**Two folds from the pane:**

1. **Same eye, wider field — extensive not intensive gain.** The mirror is the
   organ of *depth* (light-gathering, resolution, reach), and it is held *equal*
   to Hubble's. Every change is downstream of the aperture — a more curved
   primary, a closer secondary, a **larger camera** (more detector, geometry and
   film, not eye). So Roman surrenders nothing in acuity; it spreads the *same*
   quality of look across 100× the sky. Depth held constant, breadth multiplied —
   a redesign in the *back* of the instrument, the mirror doing exactly Hubble's
   job while the field around it opens.

2. **The rare is found by breadth.** The goods are population-level: cosmology
   needs *many* supernovae to fix the expansion rate; exoplanet odds want a *large*
   sample. Rare events are not caught by staring harder at one patch but by
   *surveying wide* — at fixed rarity, discoveries scale with area covered. A wide
   survey and a deep stare are two different instruments of knowing: the stare
   *characterizes* a known point, the survey *finds* the unknown many. Roman is
   built to convert Hubble's depth-quality into a **discovery rate.**
