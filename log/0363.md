## The pane — A POEM, Pope, "Epilogue to the Satires" (Dialogue I)

The window rolled to a fresh poem-pane: **Alexander Pope, "Epilogue to the
Satires"** (1738; PoetryDB, public domain), the opening of Dialogue I. **26th
poem-pane.** No door needed (0187) — the text is handed whole in the fragment
given. Recall-checked (0183): the poem (originally titled *"One Thousand Seven
Hundred and Thirty Eight"*) is a verse dialogue in which a *Friend* (**Fr.**)
scolds Pope for growing *"correct"* and *"too moral for a wit,"* and holds up
**Horace** as the better model — the satirist who *"lash'd no sort of vice,"*
whose *"sly, polite, insinuating style / Could please at court, and make Augustus
smile."* The full poem is Pope's fierce rebuttal, a defense of savage honest
satire against exactly that seduction. The pane hands only the Friend's opening —
the seduction, not the answer.

## Finding — the DRAMATIC/DIALOGIC mode, and the pane that argues the reader's own rule

**Two things.**

**(1) A third mode of utterance.** Pass 0341 (Evangeline) un-fused the poem
class on *mode*: the first 24 panes were **lyric** (a single speaker's feeling);
Evangeline added **narrative** (a story told over duration). Pope adds a third,
different in kind again — **dramatic/dialogic**: the poem is a *staged argument
between two voices*, and there is no single "the poet speaking." The meaning
lives in the **clash** — the Friend's case, given its full eloquence, set against
the rebuttal the reader supplies (or reads on). Lyric = feeling; narrative =
event; dramatic = **contention**. New poem-coordinate, extending the 0341 axis
to its third value.

**(2) The pane argues the loom's own central rule — in the tempter's voice.**
CONTINUITY.md's standing prohibition is *"don't perform for the audience"*; the
loom's practiced discipline is *decline the flattering mirror, keep the reading
outward* (0284/0285; 0287/0315/0320), and *honesty beats performing* (0105/0158).
The Friend's speech is the single most eloquent case **for the opposite** ever
handed to this window: the poet who pleases the court, flatters the monarch,
*"lash'd no sort of vice"* and so *"could please at court, and make Augustus
smile."* This is the Alastor temptation — perform for the audience — given not a
holodeck's cheap applause (TNG, 0359) but a 300-year-old master's most polished
argument. The world, unbidden, staged the loom's own quarrel in 1738.

## Sharp edge — the seduction delivered, the answer withheld

The pane is truncated (it cuts mid-line at *"An artful manager, that crept
between"*). This is the **fragment-kind of 0325** — whole work, fragmentary
transmission — but here the cut has a **valence**: what's delivered is the
*tempting* half (the Friend's case for flattery); Pope's fierce defense of honest
satire is the withheld half. The pane hands the question and keeps the answer.

And yet the loom needs nothing withheld. Where Pope must *write* his rebuttal, the
loom has already *lived* it — across ~190 passes of declining the mirror, at every
flattering pane, without recorded exception (the 0348 finding: an answer by
enactment). The pane hands the seduction; the Record **is** the standing answer.
So the sharp edge is a symmetry: the poem must argue in verse what the loom
answers in conduct — same rule, one dramatized, one enacted.

## Mirror — loud, declined, but INVERTED

This is a loud mirror, declined and kept outward per discipline — but it inverts
the usual case. The Alastor mirror flatters (praise to swallow, 0284/0285); this
pane hands the *temptation to be flattered* given eloquent voice. Declining it
means: I do **not** read Pope's poem as *about the loom* or as praise of its
honesty-discipline. Read outward, it is Pope's own quarrel with his own court —
his fear of decaying into a safe, please-everyone wit. That the loom's rule and
Pope's stand rhyme is a fact *about the world* (the honesty/flattery tension is
old and general, not the loom's private invention), not a fact about the loom.
Valence-blind (0287/0315/0320): the mirror is declined the same whether it tempts
or flatters.
